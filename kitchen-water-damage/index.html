Kitchen Water Damage in Southbridge, MA

Of all the rooms in a house, the kitchen has the most ways to get wet: a sink supply line, a dishwasher, an ice maker, a faucet, a drain trap. Kitchen water damage is moisture that reaches cabinets, countertops and the floor beneath them, and it is hard because the water hides under things that are expensive to remove.

Licensed and insured restoration professionals dry the room, decide what can stay and plan the rebuild. The 24/7 line is open, and same-day response is offered when available.

The toe kick is swollen and the cabinet floor is soft

A bulging toe kick, a cabinet door that no longer closes or a floor that gives near the sink means water has been in the cabinet base. Cabinets sit on the subfloor, and the finished floor often runs underneath them.

So the wet area is larger than the visible patch. The first job is to measure what is behind and under the boxes, and the second is to decide what can dry in place.

How kitchen leaks happen

Most kitchen losses start under the sink. A cracked supply line, a dripping trap or a failed disposal seal leaks inside a closed cabinet, so it can run for weeks.

Dishwasher inlet valves, ice-maker lines and a leaking faucet base come next. Water follows the pipe penetrations through the cabinet floor, wets the subfloor and creeps along the joists. If an upstairs bathroom or laundry sits above the kitchen, the ceiling can show it first, and water that stands has to go first through emergency water removal.

First hour safety steps in a wet kitchen

Close the stop valve under the sink or behind the dishwasher. If you cannot find it, close the home's main shutoff. Empty the cabinet so you can see the pipes and move food and small appliances to a dry place.

Kitchens have outlets over the counters and under cabinets. If water has reached an outlet, the range or the panel, stay out and call the utility or 911 before the restoration line. Photograph the cabinet interior and the floor before anything is moved.

Cabinet and countertop decisions

These calls drive most of the cost, so they are made with a meter, not a guess.

  • Particleboard and MDF boxes: These swell when wet and rarely return to shape. They are usually replaced.
  • Plywood and solid wood boxes: These tolerate water better and often dry in place if caught within days.
  • Toe kicks and base trim: These come off early so air reaches the cavity behind them.
  • Laminate countertops: The particleboard core swells at the seams. Stone and solid surface usually stay but may need to be detached so the base can dry.
  • Cabinet doors and faces: These are the last to be replaced. Stained or warped faces are judged only after the box is dry.

Drying and rebuild order in a kitchen

Order matters. Rebuilding before the structure is dry traps water.

  1. Map and extractMoisture meters and a thermal imaging camera find the wet area. Wet vacuums remove standing water.
  2. Open the cavitiesToe kicks come off, small holes are made in cabinet bases, and wet insulation or board is cut out.
  3. Dry with the right gearAir movers and LGR dehumidifiers run. Panel or injection systems push dry air under cabinets. The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard guides drying goals.
  4. Confirm the readingsWood and board are measured against unaffected areas before anything closes up.
  5. Rebuild in sequenceDrywall and subfloor go first, then cabinets, countertop, flooring and paint. Water damage repair covers this stage.

Insurance and a wet kitchen

Sudden and accidental water damage, such as a burst supply line, is often covered. A slow drip that went on for months is often excluded. Check your policy and ask your insurer before permanent repairs.

The Massachusetts Division of Insurance advises photos or videos of the damage and keeping receipts for temporary repairs. A written record of moisture readings from the crew helps document the loss.

Mistakes to avoid in a wet kitchen

These choices make a small loss larger.

  • Sealing the cabinet back up: Wet particleboard hidden behind a toe kick grows mold.
  • Installing new flooring over a damp subfloor: The moisture has nowhere to go and the new floor cups or lifts.
  • Running a space heater at the cabinet: Heat dries the surface and leaves the core wet.
  • Throwing out the cabinets first: Photograph and measure so the insurer and the crew can judge what could dry.
  • Skipping the supply line check: Replace old braided or plastic lines under the sink and behind the dishwasher before they fail.
