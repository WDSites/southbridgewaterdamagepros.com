What storms do to a house in central Massachusetts

Storm water rarely uses one door. Wind strips shingles, a fallen limb opens the roof, gutters overflow behind the fascia, and the ground outside soaks until the foundation lets water in. By morning the attic, a wall and the basement can all be wet from the same night.

The town's 2024 draft hazard mitigation plan notes that Massachusetts typically sees 10 to 15 severe thunderstorm days a year. It defines a severe thunderstorm as gusts over 58 mph or hail 1 inch or larger. Gusts of that strength can take off shingle tabs, so a roof check after any summer storm is a good habit.

Nor'easters, snow load and what the record shows

The same plan counts 33 winter storms since 1958 that produced at least 10 inches of snow in Southbridge. Its highest-scoring snowstorm is the one on 3/12/1993, rated 13.2 on the NESIS scale, which the plan calls Extreme. Heavy wet snow loads a roof and then melts fast, so the leak often arrives with the thaw, not the storm.

FEMA records show Worcester County was part of the October 2011 Massachusetts severe storm and snowstorm declaration, which listed an incident period of Oct 29 to 30. That is a county designation, not a Southbridge damage count. The practical point is that early snow with leaves still on trees snaps limbs, and limbs open roofs.

Safety checklist after a storm: power lines, generators and wet circuits

OSHA says not to touch downed power lines or any object or water in contact with them. Do not turn power back on until a qualified electrician has inspected wet equipment. Call the utility or 911 about lines and gas before the restoration line.

If the power is out, run a portable generator outdoors, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents. The CDC warns that carbon monoxide kills without warning. Never connect a generator to house wiring without an approved automatic-interrupt device, because it can send power back onto lines.

What you see after a storm, and what it means

Storm damage hides well. These are the signs people report, with the likely cause.

  • A ceiling stain that appeared overnight: wind-driven rain got past flashing or a lifted shingle and soaked the attic insulation.
  • Water at the foot of a basement wall: groundwater is pushing in through the wall joint or the sump pit, a common result of days of saturated soil.
  • A sump pump running nonstop, or silent: it is overwhelmed, or the power failed and the pump stopped.
  • Wet drywall below a window: wind-driven rain passed the sash or the siding seal.
  • A damp smell in a closet or attic days later: water is still inside the cavity and nothing has dried it.

Photograph each spot before moving anything, and note the time you first saw it.

Is storm water clean? How the category is decided

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard sorts water into three categories. The IICRC says weather-driven water is not automatically Category 3, and the restorer makes the first call on site.

Rain through a roof or windowOften Category 1 or 2Insulation and drywall may dry if caught early
Groundwater seeping into a basementOften Category 2 or 3Carpet and pad are usually removed
Sewer or drain backup during a stormCategory 3Porous materials removed, see sewage backup cleanup

Time matters because water that sits can move up a category.

How a storm damage job runs

The crew works from the outside in, because stopping new water comes before drying.

  1. Confirm it is safe to enterPower, gas and any hanging ceiling are checked first. Protective gear goes on for Category 2 and 3 water.
  2. Extract standing waterTruck-mounted or portable extraction units pull water from floors and sumps. This overlaps with emergency water removal.
  3. Map and removeA moisture meter and thermal imaging camera show the wet zones. Soaked pad, insulation and low drywall are cut out.
  4. Dry and monitorAir movers and LGR dehumidifiers run, and readings are logged until they reach the dry standard. Details are on water mitigation.

Which policy covers which storm loss

Wind and rain that come through a storm-damaged roof are usually handled under a homeowners policy. Groundwater coming through a basement wall is flood, and the Massachusetts Division of Insurance says standard homeowners policies do not cover flood damage. A rider may add sump pump failure or water backup.

The state's post-disaster claims guide says to photograph damage, make only essential temporary repairs, keep receipts and talk to your insurer before permanent repairs. Check your policy, and call the insurer early.

Storm mistakes to avoid

A few habits make storm losses worse.

  • Entering a flooded basement with power on: water and live circuits can kill.
  • Letting soaked rooms sit for a week: the EPA says to dry water-damaged areas within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth.
  • Running a generator in the garage: carbon monoxide builds up even with the door open.
  • Skipping the attic check: wet insulation hides above a ceiling that still looks fine.
