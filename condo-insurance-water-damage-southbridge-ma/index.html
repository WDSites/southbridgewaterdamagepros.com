Condo Insurance and Water Damage: Who Pays in Southbridge, MA?

A condo has two layers of insurance, and water damage usually lands on one layer or the other depending on where the water started and what it touched.

By the Southbridge Water Damage Pros team · 2026

Call (508) 446-9244

The short answer: it depends on two documents

The association's master policy generally covers the building and common areas. Your own unit owner policy, often called an HO-6, generally covers what is inside your walls and what you are responsible for under the bylaws. Which one pays for a given loss turns on the cause, the location of the damage and the exact wording of both policies.

We cannot tell you which policy applies to your unit. Only your documents, your association and your insurer can. No company can promise a claim will be approved, and restoration crews dry, clean and repair rather than decide coverage. If water has already reached finishes, water damage cleanup is the next step after drying.

Master policy and HO-6 side by side

These are general patterns, not a reading of any specific policy. Associations draw the line in different places, so treat the table as a list of questions rather than answers.

The bottom row matters most for owners near a river. Standard policies commonly leave flood out, and the next sections explain what that means for a condo.

When a neighbor's leak damages your unit

Picture a second-floor unit where a washing machine hose lets go while the owner is at work. Water runs through the floor, and by evening the ceiling in the unit below is stained, sagging and dripping onto a hardwood floor.

Who pays can depend on several things. Was the source inside the upstairs unit or in a shared pipe? Does the owner above carry liability coverage? Does the master policy treat your ceiling drywall as building or as interior finish? Your HO-6 may pay for your belongings and finishes first, and the insurers may sort out responsibility between themselves afterward.

The practical move is to call your insurer and your association manager the same day, not to argue about fault while the wall stays wet. Questions of blame can wait. Drying cannot.

What to do in the first day

Condo owners lose time when they wait to learn whose problem it is. This order keeps both the damage and the paperwork under control.

  1. Stop what you canIf the source is in your unit, shut the local supply valve or the unit's main. If it comes from above, tell the association so the shared line can be closed.
  2. Photograph before you move anythingTake wide shots of each room and close shots of stains, wet carpet and affected furniture. Add the date to your notes.
  3. Notify the association and your insurerAsk the manager which policy the association believes applies, and ask your insurer what your HO-6 requires for reporting a claim.
  4. Start dryingCall the 24/7 line for water mitigation so air movers and dehumidifiers go in before wet drywall and insulation start to degrade. Keep every receipt.
  5. Ask for the documentsRequest the master policy summary, the bylaws and the declarations page of your HO-6. Read the sections on interior walls, water damage and deductibles.

Questions to put to your association and insurer

Ask these in writing when you can, and keep the answers with your photos.

  • Where is the line between building and unit? Some associations insure bare walls only. Others insure finished interiors as originally built.
  • What is the master policy deductible, and who pays it? Some bylaws push the deductible onto the owner whose unit the loss started in.
  • Does my HO-6 cover damage from a neighbor's leak? Many do for belongings and finishes, but limits and exclusions vary.
  • Is there loss assessment coverage? This add-on can help if the association bills owners for a shared loss.
  • Does the policy cover water backup from a drain? Backup is often an optional rider, so check your policy and ask your insurer.
  • What counts as sudden and accidental? Policies commonly treat a burst hose differently from a slow leak that ran for weeks.

Flood coverage: renters and owners can both buy it

Rising water from a river or heavy storm is a separate matter. Standard condo, homeowner and renter policies commonly exclude flood, and FEMA says most standard homeowners policies do not cover it. Flood insurance is a different product, and a condo owner or tenant can buy it for their own unit and contents.

If you rent in a building near the Quinebaug River, your landlord's policy does not protect your furniture and clothing. A renter can carry flood coverage for contents alone, and ground-floor and basement storage is where most of the risk sits.

Shared walls make early calls matter

In any building where several units share a plumbing stack, one leaking line can wet two or three units at once. That is the case where a same-day call to the association helps everyone.

The same questions apply whether the building is in Sturbridge or Oxford, and the insurance questions above do not change by town.

What NFIP availability means for a Southbridge condo owner

The Massachusetts Division of Insurance states that standard homeowners and commercial business policies do not cover flood damage. It also says National Flood Insurance Program coverage is available to renters, homeowners and business owners alike, so a condo tenant is not shut out. Southbridge participates in the NFIP, per the town's hazard mitigation plan, which is the condition that makes those policies available here.

The practical catch is timing. According to the state guidance, NFIP policies usually have a 30-day waiting period, with exceptions for loan-related purchases. A policy bought the day a storm is forecast generally will not respond to that storm.

For a condo owner, that means the time to ask is now. Check the FEMA flood map for your address, then ask your insurer or agent whether an NFIP or private flood policy fits your unit and contents.
